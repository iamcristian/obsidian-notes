### Bite the bullet
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** hacer de tripas corazón; afrontar una decisión o tarea difícil con entereza
- **Definition:** To force oneself to accept and deal with an unpleasant, difficult, or unavoidable situation without further hesitation.
- **Example:** *"After months of debate, the team decided to bite the bullet and rewrite the billing module."*
- **Collocations:** *finally bite the bullet; bite the bullet and + base verb; near-synonym: face the music*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Natural in spoken English, workplace communication, and journalism; avoid in highly formal academic prose (use 'take decisive action' or 'confront the issue').
- **Word Family:** bullet (bala); from 19th-century accounts of wounded soldiers clenching a lead bullet during surgery

### Cut corners
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** escatimar esfuerzos o recursos; hacer algo de forma chapucera por ahorrar tiempo o dinero
- **Definition:** To do something in the quickest, easiest, or cheapest way possible, usually by skipping essential steps and sacrificing quality or safety.
- **Example:** *"Inspectors concluded that the contractor had cut corners on fireproofing to stay under budget."*
- **Collocations:** *cut corners on + noun; refuse to cut corners; corner-cutting (noun/adj)*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Almost always carries negative criticism; Cambridge C1 trap: do not confuse with 'round the corner' (nearby/imminent).
- **Word Family:** corner (esquina/atajo); literal metaphor of taking a diagonal shortcut instead of following the proper path

### Burn the midnight oil
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** quemarse las pestañas; trasnochar trabajando o estudiando
- **Definition:** To work or study late into the night, especially to meet a demanding deadline or prepare thoroughly.
- **Example:** *"With the product launch moved forward by two weeks, the engineers have been burning the midnight oil."*
- **Collocations:** *be burning the midnight oil; compare: burn the candle at both ends (overwork day and night)*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Slightly literary yet common in C1 speaking and journalism; implies dedication rather than disorganization.
- **Word Family:** midnight oil (aceite de lámpara de medianoche); from working late by oil-lamp light before electricity

### Pull one's weight
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** arrimar el hombro; cumplir con la parte de trabajo que le corresponde a uno
- **Definition:** To contribute a fair share of effort or responsibility within a group, team, or organization.
- **Example:** *"The project only succeeded because every single member pulled their weight during the final sprint."*
- **Collocations:** *fail to pull one's weight; make sure everyone pulls their weight; antonym: coast / slack off*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Very frequently used in the negative ('not pulling his weight'); always requires a possessive matching the subject.
- **Word Family:** weight (peso/carga); from rowing crews where each rower must propel at least their own body weight

### Go the extra mile
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** hacer un esfuerzo adicional; dar más de lo que se exige
- **Definition:** To make a special, voluntary effort beyond what is strictly expected or required to achieve an excellent result.
- **Example:** *"Clients stay loyal to consultants who consistently go the extra mile when a crisis hits."*
- **Collocations:** *willing to go the extra mile; go the extra mile for + someone; synonym: go above and beyond*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Widely accepted in cover letters, performance reviews, and Cambridge Speaking interviews; avoid overusing in formal essays.
- **Word Family:** mile (milla); from the biblical injunction to walk a second mile when compelled to walk one

### Call it a day
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** dar la jornada por terminada; dejar de trabajar por hoy o retirarse de una actividad
- **Definition:** To decide to stop working or pursuing an activity, either for the rest of the day or permanently.
- **Example:** *"We have cleared the critical bugs, so let's call it a day and resume testing tomorrow morning."*
- **Collocations:** *let's call it a day; call it a night (evening); decide to call it a day (retire/quit)*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Can also mean retiring from a career ('After twenty years in finance, she called it a day'); never say 'call it the day'.
- **Word Family:** day (jornada); originally 'call it half a day' among 19th-century workers knocking off early

### Learn the ropes
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** aprender los entresijos del oficio; familiarizarse con las rutinas básicas de un puesto
- **Definition:** To learn the basic procedures, rules, and practical details needed to do a new job or activity effectively.
- **Example:** *"It takes most analysts at least three months to learn the ropes of our regulatory reporting system."*
- **Collocations:** *show someone the ropes (teach); know the ropes (be experienced); still learning the ropes*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Focuses on practical operational know-how during onboarding; 'know the ropes' describes mastery, whereas 'learn the ropes' describes the initial phase.
- **Word Family:** ropes (cabos/cuerdas); from sailing, where new deckhands had to memorize which rigging lines controlled each sail

### Get the hang of something
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal]
- **Español:** cogerle el truco o el tranquillo a algo
- **Definition:** To acquire the knack, intuitive understanding, or practical skill required to do something smoothly.
- **Example:** *"Keyboard shortcuts feel awkward at first, but once you get the hang of them, your editing speed doubles."*
- **Collocations:** *get the hang of + noun/-ing; finally get the hang of it; once you get the hang of...*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Strictly conversational/semi-informal; never pluralize 'hang' and always follow with 'of + noun/-ing'.
- **Word Family:** hang (maña/forma de agarrar); from 19th-century American usage referring to how a tool hangs or balances in the hand

### Back to the drawing board
?
- **Part of Speech:** Adverbial Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** volver a empezar de cero; replantear un proyecto tras un fracaso
- **Definition:** Used to indicate that an existing plan or prototype has failed and a completely new approach must be designed from the beginning.
- **Example:** *"When the prototype failed the stress test, the engineering team went straight back to the drawing board."*
- **Collocations:** *go back to the drawing board; it's back to the drawing board; start from scratch*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Often used predicatively ('It's back to the drawing board') or after 'go'; appropriate in business reporting and speaking.
- **Word Family:** drawing board (mesa de dibujo); from architectural and engineering drafting tables

### Miss the boat
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** perder el tren; dejar escapar una oportunidad por actuar demasiado tarde
- **Definition:** To lose an opportunity by failing to act quickly enough or by overlooking the right moment.
- **Example:** *"Retailers that ignored e-commerce in the early 2000s completely missed the boat."*
- **Collocations:** *completely miss the boat; miss the boat on + noun; synonym: miss the bus (BrE)*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Spanish speakers often translate 'perder el tren' literally as 'miss the train'; in English, 'miss the boat' is the standard figurative idiom.
- **Word Family:** boat (barco); literal image of arriving at the pier after the vessel has already departed

### Burn the candle at both ends
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** quemar las naves de la salud; agotarse trabajando sin descanso día y noche
- **Definition:** To exhaust oneself by working excessively hard or keeping late nights and early mornings without adequate rest.
- **Example:** *"Juggling a full-time consultancy role and an evening MBA meant she was burning the candle at both ends."*
- **Collocations:** *burn the candle at both ends for + months; risk burnout; compare: burn the midnight oil*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Always carries a warning about unsustainable energy expenditure and impending burnout, unlike 'burn the midnight oil' which can be temporary.
- **Word Family:** candle (vela); lighting both wicks at once doubles brightness while halving the candle's lifespan

### Hit the ground running
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** empezar con el pie derecho y a pleno rendimiento; arrancar con energía desde el minuto uno
- **Definition:** To begin a new job, project, or phase immediately at full speed and effectiveness without needing a slow warm-up.
- **Example:** *"Because she already knew the codebase, our new tech lead was able to hit the ground running."*
- **Collocations:** *ready to hit the ground running; need someone who can hit the ground running*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Extremely common in recruitment, business English, and C1 interviews; note 'ground' is singular and 'running' is a fixed participle.
- **Word Family:** hit the ground (tocar suelo); from paratroopers or train-jumpers landing already in motion

### Pull out all the stops
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** echar toda la carne en el asador; no escatimar esfuerzos ni recursos
- **Definition:** To use every available resource, skill, and effort to ensure something succeeds impressively.
- **Example:** *"The hospitality team pulled out all the stops to make the international summit run seamlessly."*
- **Collocations:** *pull out all the stops to + verb; pull out all the stops for + event*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Positive and energetic; a frequent Cambridge Use of English trap is replacing 'stops' with 'tricks' or 'efforts'.
- **Word Family:** stops (registros de órgano); from pipe organs, where pulling out every stop lets air through all pipes at maximum volume

### Leave no stone unturned
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Formal/Neutral]
- **Español:** no dejar piedra sin mover; agotar todas las vías posibles en una búsqueda o investigación
- **Definition:** To explore every conceivable option, clue, or course of action in order to solve a problem or find something.
- **Example:** *"Auditors pledged to leave no stone unturned until the missing funds were accounted for."*
- **Collocations:** *leave no stone unturned in + -ing / search for; pledge to leave no stone unturned*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** One of the few idioms elevated enough for formal reports, investigative journalism, and C1/C2 essays.
- **Word Family:** stone (piedra); from an ancient Greek oracle advising a general to turn over every stone to locate buried treasure

### Move the goalposts
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** cambiar las reglas a mitad del partido; alterar los criterios injustamente
- **Definition:** To unfairly change the rules, targets, or conditions of an agreement or task while it is already underway.
- **Example:** *"Just as the researchers met the initial safety threshold, regulators moved the goalposts again."*
- **Collocations:** *constantly move the goalposts; accuse someone of moving the goalposts; shift the goalposts*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Strongly critical; in Cambridge C1 writing/speaking, useful when discussing unfair policy shifts or scope creep.
- **Word Family:** goalposts (portería/postes); from football/rugby, where shifting the posts mid-play makes scoring impossible

### Keep one's nose to the grindstone
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** hincar los codos; trabajar con constancia y sin distracciones
- **Definition:** To work steadily, diligently, and without distraction for a prolonged period.
- **Example:** *"If we keep our noses to the grindstone until Friday, we should clear the backlog before the audit."*
- **Collocations:** *keep/put one's nose to the grindstone; plural subjects take 'noses to the grindstone'*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Emphasizes disciplined, unglamorous persistence rather than inspiration; check plural agreement ('their noses').
- **Word Family:** grindstone (piedra de afilar); from bladesmiths leaning closely over a sharpening wheel

### Toe the line
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** pasar por el aro; acatar las normas o la disciplina establecida
- **Definition:** To obey rules, standards, or the wishes of someone in authority, often reluctantly or under institutional pressure.
- **Example:** *"Dissident MPs were warned to toe the party line or lose their committee seats."*
- **Collocations:** *toe the party line; force someone to toe the line; refuse to toe the line*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Common spelling trap in Cambridge exams: it is 'toe' (dedo del pie), NEVER 'tow the line' (remolcar).
- **Word Family:** toe (punta del pie); from soldiers or runners lining their toes along a starting or inspection line

### Bend over backwards
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** desvivirse por alguien; hacer lo imposible para complacer o ayudar
- **Definition:** To make an extraordinary effort to help, accommodate, or satisfy someone, often at personal inconvenience.
- **Example:** *"The support staff bent over backwards to arrange emergency visas for the visiting speakers."*
- **Collocations:** *bend over backwards to + verb; bend over backwards for + someone; BrE variant: lean over backwards*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Often highlights either admirable generosity or frustration when extreme accommodation goes unappreciated.
- **Word Family:** bend backwards (arquear la espalda); literal image of extreme physical flexibility

### Pass the buck
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** escurrir el bulto; pasarle la responsabilidad o culpa a otro
- **Definition:** To shift responsibility or blame for a problem onto someone else instead of dealing with it oneself.
- **Example:** *"When the outage occurred, both vendors tried to pass the buck instead of diagnosing the server logs."*
- **Collocations:** *stop passing the buck; buck-passing (noun); related: the buck stops here (final responsibility)*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Always critical in tone; contrast with Harry Truman's famous 'The buck stops here' meaning ultimate accountability.
- **Word Family:** buck (ficha/marcador); from 19th-century poker, where a buckhorn-handled knife marked the next dealer

### Drop the ball
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal]
- **Español:** meter la pata por descuido; fallar en una responsabilidad clave
- **Definition:** To make a careless mistake or fail to carry out a duty or task that others were relying on you to handle.
- **Example:** *"We lost the account because someone dropped the ball on renewing the compliance certificate."*
- **Collocations:** *drop the ball on + noun; really drop the ball; contrast: keep one's eye on the ball*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Mostly spoken and workplace-informal; implies negligence or lost focus rather than deliberate sabotage.
- **Word Family:** ball (pelota); from baseball, rugby, or American football fumbles costing the team a play

### Get the ball rolling
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** poner la maquinaria en marcha; dar el primer paso para iniciar algo
- **Definition:** To initiate a process, project, or conversation so that momentum begins to build.
- **Example:** *"Let's circulate the draft agenda today to get the ball rolling before next week's workshop."*
- **Collocations:** *start/set/keep the ball rolling; get the ball rolling on + noun*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Very natural in meetings and emails; avoid using it with 'ball' pluralized.
- **Word Family:** ball rolling (pelota rodando); from ball games or literal momentum once an object starts moving

### Go through the motions
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** cumplir el expediente; hacer algo por pura rutina sin interés ni entusiasmo
- **Definition:** To perform a task mechanically out of habit or obligation, without genuine commitment, energy, or care.
- **Example:** *"Burnout had set in, and half the department seemed to be merely going through the motions."*
- **Collocations:** *just/merely going through the motions; accuse someone of going through the motions*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Ideal C1 expression for essays on workplace disengagement or education; always carries a critical tone of detachment.
- **Word Family:** motions (movimientos/gestos); performing the external gestures without real substance behind them

### Put something on the back burner
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** dejar algo en segundo plano; aparcar temporalmente un asunto por falta de prioridad
- **Definition:** To postpone work on a task, plan, or issue temporarily so that more urgent matters can be addressed first.
- **Example:** *"Due to staffing shortages, we had to put the office redesign on the back burner until Q3."*
- **Collocations:** *put/leave/push something on the back burner; sit on the back burner; antonym: move to the front burner*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Implies deferral rather than permanent cancellation; contrast with 'shelve' or 'scrap'.
- **Word Family:** back burner (fogón trasero); from cooking on a stove, keeping simmering pots at the rear while tending urgent dishes in front

### Race against the clock
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** luchar contra reloj; trabajar contrarreloj bajo un plazo límite muy ajustado
- **Definition:** To work as fast as possible because there is a strict and rapidly approaching deadline.
- **Example:** *"Paramedics and engineers were in a race against the clock to restore power before nightfall."*
- **Collocations:** *be in a race against the clock; race against time; work against the clock*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Works both as verb phrase ('race against the clock') and noun phrase ('a race against the clock'); safe across journalism and C1 writing.
- **Word Family:** clock (reloj); competing directly with the ticking seconds of a countdown

### Down to the wire
?
- **Part of Speech:** Adverbial Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** hasta el último minuto; al filo del plazo límite
- **Definition:** Continuing right up to the very last moment before a deadline or decision, often with intense uncertainty.
- **Example:** *"Contract negotiations went right down to the wire before the union approved the final offer."*
- **Collocations:** *go/come down to the wire; right down to the wire; come in under the wire (just before the deadline)*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Frequently used in business, sports, and politics; note the verbs 'go' or 'come' precede it naturally.
- **Word Family:** wire (alambre de meta); from 19th-century horse racing, where a wire was stretched across the finish line

### Crunch time
?
- **Part of Speech:** Nominal Idiom [Informal]
- **Español:** la hora de la verdad; el momento crítico de máxima presión
- **Definition:** A crucial, high-pressure period shortly before a deadline when decisive action or intense effort is required.
- **Example:** *"Now that the final audit starts Monday, it is officially crunch time for the finance team."*
- **Collocations:** *be crunch time for + someone; when crunch time comes; reach crunch time*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Informal and punchy; great in spoken narratives and tech/business journalism, too colloquial for academic essays.
- **Word Family:** crunch (momento decisivo/aprieto); from sports and business jargon for decisive pressure points

### Cut one's teeth on something
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** curtirse en algo; adquirir la primera experiencia práctica en un ámbito
- **Definition:** To gain one's earliest foundational experience and practical skill in a particular field or type of work.
- **Example:** *"Many prominent investigative journalists cut their teeth on local council reporting."*
- **Collocations:** *cut one's teeth on + noun/-ing; cut one's teeth as + role; cut one's teeth in + field*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Often paired with 'as' or 'in' for roles/sectors and 'on' for tasks/projects; refers strictly to early-career formation.
- **Word Family:** cut teeth (salir los dientes); literal reference to infants teething as an early developmental milestone

### Spread oneself too thin
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** quien mucho abarca poco aprieta; asumir demasiadas responsabilidades a la vez
- **Definition:** To take on too many commitments simultaneously, leaving oneself without enough time or energy to do any of them well.
- **Example:** *"By launching in five regions at once, the startup spread itself too thin and alienated early customers."*
- **Collocations:** *be spread too thin; spread oneself too thin across + projects; also 'spread one's resources too thin'*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Very useful in C1 business critiques; uses reflexive pronouns ('myself', 'itself', 'themselves') or passive 'be spread too thin'.
- **Word Family:** spread thin (untar muy fino); like spreading a small amount of butter across too large a slice of bread

### Have a lot on one's plate
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** estar hasta arriba de trabajo; tener demasiados asuntos pendientes
- **Definition:** To have a heavy workload or many pressing responsibilities and problems to manage at the same time.
- **Example:** *"With two product launches and a departmental merger, Maria already has plenty on her plate."*
- **Collocations:** *have enough/too much/plenty on one's plate; take something off someone's plate*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Polite, idiomatic way to decline extra tasks in professional settings; avoid saying 'on my dish'.
- **Word Family:** plate (plato); metaphor of piling more food onto an already full dinner plate

### Bite off more than one can chew
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** abarcar más de lo que se puede; asumir un reto que supera las propias fuerzas
- **Definition:** To take on a task or commitment that proves far larger, harder, or more demanding than one is able to handle.
- **Example:** *"Taking over both European logistics and customer support turned out to be biting off more than we could chew."*
- **Collocations:** *realize one has bitten off more than one can chew; careful not to bite off more than you can chew*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Focuses on overestimating one's capacity for a single big venture, whereas 'spread oneself too thin' emphasizes too many small tasks.
- **Word Family:** chew (masticar); literal image of taking a mouthful too large to swallow

### Wear many hats
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** desempeñar múltiples funciones; hacer de todo en una organización
- **Definition:** To hold several different roles, duties, or areas of responsibility within a workplace or organization.
- **Example:** *"In an early-stage charity, the operations director often has to wear many hats, from HR to fundraising."*
- **Collocations:** *wear several/different/two hats; wear one's + role + hat ('wearing my lawyer hat')*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Highly idiomatic in C1 job interviews and workplace discussions; can also appear as 'wear two hats' for dual roles.
- **Word Family:** hats (sombreros); from distinct uniforms or headwear historically worn for separate trades and offices

### Rise through the ranks
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** abrirse paso desde abajo; ascender progresivamente en una jerarquía
- **Definition:** To advance gradually from a junior or entry-level position to senior leadership within an organization.
- **Example:** *"Having joined as a junior technician, he rose through the ranks to become chief engineer."*
- **Collocations:** *rise through the ranks of + organization; come up through the ranks; work one's way up*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Polished and respectful register; ideal in biographies, CVs, and Cambridge C1 speaking about careers.
- **Word Family:** ranks (filas/escalafón); from military service, advancing from enlisted 'ranks' into officer grades

### Carry the can
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/BrE]
- **Español:** cargar con el muerto; asumir injustamente la culpa o el castigo por otro
- **Definition:** To take the blame or bear the responsibility for a mistake or failure, often unfairly on behalf of others.
- **Example:** *"When the marketing campaign backfired, the junior coordinator was left to carry the can."*
- **Collocations:** *be left to carry the can; carry the can for + someone/something; AmE near-equivalent: take the fall*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Predominantly British English; American speakers more often say 'take the fall' or 'be left holding the bag'.
- **Word Family:** can (lata/bidón); likely from military or industrial slang for the unpopular chore of fetching or disposing of cans

### Rest on one's laurels
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** dormirse en los laureles; conformarse con éxitos pasados sin seguir esforzándose
- **Definition:** To become complacent after past achievements and stop making the effort needed to maintain or improve success.
- **Example:** *"Despite winning market share last year, the company cannot afford to rest on its laurels."*
- **Collocations:** *cannot afford to rest on one's laurels; refuse to rest on one's laurels; sit on one's laurels (rare)*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Almost always used in negative advice or warnings; suitable for both formal business essays and speaking.
- **Word Family:** laurels (corona de laurel); from ancient Greek and Roman laurel wreaths awarded to victors

### Jump through hoops
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** pasar por mil aros; cumplir trámites burocráticos absurdos o pruebas excesivas
- **Definition:** To complete a series of frustrating, complicated, or arbitrary requirements imposed by someone in authority.
- **Example:** *"Applicants have to jump through endless hoops just to renew a standard research permit."*
- **Collocations:** *make someone jump through hoops; jump through bureaucratic hoops; jump through all the hoops*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Conveys irritation at unnecessary complexity or power imbalance; pairs naturally with 'endless' or 'bureaucratic'.
- **Word Family:** hoops (aros); from circus animals trained to leap through hoops on command

### Red tape
?
- **Part of Speech:** Nominal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** papeleo burocrático; exceso de trámites y trabas administrativas
- **Definition:** Excessive official rules, paperwork, and bureaucratic procedures that delay action or decision-making.
- **Example:** *"Small clinics struggle under mountains of red tape before receiving routine equipment approvals."*
- **Collocations:** *cut through red tape; bogged down in red tape; bureaucratic red tape; uncountable noun*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Uncountable noun—never say 'a red tape' or 'red tapes'; widely accepted in formal journalism, policy debates, and C1 essays.
- **Word Family:** red tape (cinta roja); from the crimson ribbon once used to tie British government and legal documents

### Reinvent the wheel
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Neutral]
- **Español:** inventar la pólvora / reinventar la rueda; perder el tiempo creando algo que ya existe
- **Definition:** To waste time and effort creating a solution or system that has already been developed and proven elsewhere.
- **Example:** *"Before writing a custom authentication library, check open-source options so we don't reinvent the wheel."*
- **Collocations:** *no need to reinvent the wheel; avoid reinventing the wheel; try to reinvent the wheel*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Almost exclusively used negatively ('don't reinvent the wheel', 'there's no need to reinvent the wheel').
- **Word Family:** wheel (rueda); metaphor for duplicating one of humanity's oldest solved inventions

### Spin one's wheels
?
- **Part of Speech:** Verbal Idiom [Informal/AmE]
- **Español:** dar vueltas en balde; esforzarse mucho sin avanzar nada
- **Definition:** To expend considerable time and energy without making any tangible progress.
- **Example:** *"Without clear priorities from management, the committee spent three months spinning its wheels."*
- **Collocations:** *just spinning one's wheels; feel like one is spinning one's wheels; BrE near-synonym: tread water*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Very common in North American workplace English; captures frustrated stagnation despite busyness.
- **Word Family:** spin wheels (patinar las ruedas); from vehicle tires rotating furiously in mud or ice without moving forward

### Work one's fingers to the bone
?

### Wear and tear

### Ins and outs
?

- **Español:** los entresijos y pormenores; todos los detalles internos de un proceso o asunto
- **Definition:** All the intricate details, rules, subtleties, and practical workings of a complex activity or system.

- **Word Family:** ins and outs (entradas y salidas); metaphor of knowing every doorway, corner, and twist of a structure

### Part and parcel
?
- **Part of Speech:** Binomial Pair [Formal/Neutral]
- **Español:** parte integrante e inseparable; elemento inevitable o esencial de algo
- **Definition:** An essential, unavoidable, and inseparable component of a particular experience, role, or system.

- **Word Family:** part and parcel (parte y paquete); redundant legal doublet from Anglo-French property deeds

### Null and void

### Make or break
?
- **Part of Speech:** Binomial Pair [Neutral]
- **Español:** decisivo, a vida o muerte; que determina el éxito total o el fracaso absoluto
- **Definition:** Decisive enough to bring about either complete success or total ruin, with no middle ground.

### Hit and miss
?
- **Part of Speech:** Binomial Pair [Informal/Neutral]
- **Español:** desigual, según el día; irregular e impredecible en resultados o calidad
- **Definition:** Inconsistent, unpredictable, and uneven—sometimes succeeding well and other times failing badly.
- **Example:** *"Public Wi-Fi coverage along the coastal railway remains rather hit and miss depending on tunnel depth."*
- **Collocations:** *be a bit hit and miss; a hit-and-miss affair/system (hyphenated before noun); variant: hit or miss*
- **Nuance & Exam Tip:** Both 'hit and miss' and 'hit or miss' are widely accepted; contrast with 'touch and go' (high-stakes suspense over survival/collapse).
- **Word Family:** hit and miss (acierto y fallo); marksmanship imagery of shots striking or missing a target inconsistently
